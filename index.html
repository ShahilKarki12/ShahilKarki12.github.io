Research

Master of Business Research thesis — in progress, 2026 · Excelsia University College, Sydney

Application of AI in Algorithmic Trading: From Predictive Accuracy to Profitability and Market Outcomes

Machine learning models routinely report strong accuracy on stock price prediction — yet accuracy alone doesn't pay. This research compares baseline statistical models against LSTM, GRU, and sentiment-hybrid deep learning architectures on US large-cap and S&P 500 data (2014–2024), asking a harder question: after transaction costs and realistic execution, which predictive gains survive as actual trading profitability, and what does that mean for market outcomes?

Insights

Short notes from my research and project work.

Why prediction accuracy doesn't equal trading profit

September 2026

A model that predicts tomorrow's price direction with 60% accuracy sounds like a money machine. In practice, it often isn't — and the gap between accuracy and profitability is the central question of my thesis.

Three things eat the edge. First, transaction costs: a strategy that trades often pays spreads and fees on every signal, and small per-trade edges disappear fast. Second, asymmetry: being right on many small moves and wrong on a few large ones can leave you accurate and unprofitable at the same time. Third, timing: a correct prediction that executes late is a different trade from the one the backtest assumed.

This is why my research evaluates LSTM, GRU, and sentiment-hybrid models not just on error metrics, but on what remains after realistic costs. The interesting result isn't which model predicts best — it's which predictive gains survive contact with the market.

What screening 50+ papers taught me about AI in trading

August 2026

For my literature review I filtered the field down to 50–60 papers and documented each one — abstract, method, data, and key claims. Patterns emerge quickly when you read at that volume.

Most papers stop at predictive metrics: RMSE, directional accuracy, sometimes a Sharpe ratio from a frictionless backtest. Far fewer model transaction costs, and fewer still ask what happens to markets when these strategies deploy at scale. Deep learning models — LSTMs especially — dominate recent work, but comparisons against simple baselines are often thin, which flatters the complex models.

The gap in the literature is exactly where accuracy meets economics, and that's the space my thesis works in. If you're starting a similar review: log your search strings, keep a synthesis matrix from day one, and read the methodology sections first.
